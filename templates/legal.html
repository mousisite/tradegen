This describes what Orenth actually does with data. It is written to be checkable against the source rather than to be comprehensive, and where a thing does not happen it says so plainly instead of staying silent.

What is collected

If you sign in with Google, Orenth receives and stores:

  • Your email address, used as the identity of your account.
  • Your name, and the address of your Google profile picture, used to show who is signed in. The picture itself stays at Google: a page that shows it fetches it from Google.
  • A Google account identifier, so that the account still resolves if your email address changes.

That is all Orenth gets from Google. It asks Google only for the openid, email and profile scopes. It cannot read your Gmail, Drive, contacts or calendar, and it never sees your Google password.

{% if apple_native %}

If you sign in with Apple, Orenth receives and stores:

  • An Apple account identifier, which is how Apple tells Orenth it is you again. It is made for Orenth alone.
  • An email address, used as the identity of your account. If you choose to hide yours, it is a private address from Apple, usually at privaterelay.appleid.com, that Apple forwards to your own inbox, and Orenth never sees the real one.
  • Your name, which Apple shares only the first time you sign in here, and only if you let it.
  • A token from Apple, kept only so that deleting your account can ask Apple to end Orenth's access to your Apple Account.

Orenth asks Apple only for your name and email address, and it never sees your Apple Account password.

{% endif %}

If you sign in with a code instead:

  • By email: your email address, used as the identity of your account. The code is kept only as a keyed hash. It works for ten minutes and only once.
  • By text: your phone number, used as the identity of your account. The code is created and checked by Twilio and never stored here.
  • For either, the time of each code request and the network address it came from, kept for 30 days so that nobody can flood an inbox or a phone with codes, or keep guessing at one. Deleting your account deletes them.

There are no passwords, so there are none to leak.

However you sign in, each sign-in is kept with the description your browser or the app gives of itself (its User-Agent), such as which browser and phone it is, until you sign out or it runs out after {{ session_days }} days.

If you subscribe to Pro{% if iap_on %} on the website{% endif %}, Orenth stores the customer number Stripe gives it and whether your plan is active. It never sees or stores your card.

{% if iap_on %}

If you subscribe to Pro in the iPhone app, Apple takes the payment, through your Apple Account, and Orenth never sees your card or your Apple Account. Orenth keeps what Apple signs and sends about the subscription: its transaction numbers, which plan it is, when it was bought, renews or ends, whether it was refunded, and an anonymous account token. That token is a random number Orenth made for your account, which the app hands to Apple with each purchase so that renewals and restored purchases reach the right account; it says nothing about who you are. If a subscription is moved from one Orenth account to another, Orenth keeps which account it left and when, and emails that account to say so. If you delete your account, the token is deleted. The subscription's transaction numbers, plan, dates and any refund are kept, linked to no account, until the time it paid for has run out: a copy of a purchase stays valid, and this record is what stops a refunded one giving Pro again.

{% endif %}

If you press Share on an analysis, Orenth makes a public page with that stock, the call, its counts and the date. It does not show your name or email. Deleting your account deletes these pages.

Your invite link records who joined through it, so both of you can be given the free week. If you turn on the weekly email, Orenth sends it to your sign-in address every Monday until you turn it off, with a link in every one to stop it. If you ask for alert emails on the Monitor page, each alert that fires and each position that crosses its stop is emailed to that address too, until you switch them off there.

Orenth also keeps a daily count of a few things done on the site: front page visits by people not signed in, new accounts, first analyses, watchlist additions, alerts set, paper trades and upgrades to Pro. These are counts only: a day, what happened and how many times. No account, address, ticker or network address is kept with them, so they cannot be traced back to you.

{# Inside the iPhone app nothing names another phone's system (App Store rule 2.3.10), and the subscribe screen links here. #}

If you turn on notifications on a phone or a computer, Orenth keeps what that device's push service gave it to reach the device (for a browser, a web address and two keys; for the iPhone app, a device token), a short name such as "{{ 'iPhone app' if in_app else 'Chrome on Android' }}", and when a notification last went through or why it failed. When one of your alerts fires, or a position in your journal crosses its stop, its text and the time of its price go to the device through that push service: Apple's for iPhones, iPads and Safari, Google's for Chrome{% if not in_app %} and Android{% endif %}, Mozilla's for Firefox and Microsoft's for Edge. For a stop, the text names the stock, its price, the stop and whether your position is long or short. A browser's notifications are encrypted for that browser, so the push service carries them without being able to read them; only a short label for which alert or position one is about travels unencrypted, so that a newer one can replace it. Notifications to the Orenth iPhone app pass through Apple's service, which can read them, as it can every app's. Turning notifications off on a device, removing it on the Settings page, signing out on it, or deleting your account removes the device here.

What you create

Anything you enter is stored against your account: trades, watchlist, alerts and the alerts that fired, saved theses, your settings (such as your account size and risk per trade), and the history of analyses you have run. For a free account, which stocks and coins it opened on each day is kept too, which is how its daily allowance is counted. Only you can see it. Other people signed into the same installation cannot read it, and that separation is enforced by the database query rather than by the interface hiding things.

One thing is deliberately shared: the measured strategy record, which says how a given strategy has behaved on a given instrument. It contains no personal information, describes markets rather than people, and is what makes the analysis better as more of it accumulates.

Where data goes

To produce an analysis, Orenth sends the instrument you asked about to the sources it reads. It does not send your identity, your positions or your journal to any of them.

A chart screenshot you upload is the exception: the picture itself goes to Anthropic, which reads off it which instrument it shows. Whatever else is in the picture goes with it, so crop out anything you would not want to share. It is not stored here. Nothing is sent until you tick the box under the picture area to agree, once; Settings shows when you did and takes it back.

What does not happen

  • No advertising, and no advertising identifiers.
  • No tracking scripts. The pages load no third-party JavaScript at all. Visits are counted only while you are not signed in, as described above, with no cookie, and never in the iPhone app.
  • No selling or renting of personal data, and no sharing of it beyond the services listed above, for the jobs listed there.
  • No tracking cookies. Orenth's own cookie keeps you signed in. It holds a random session token and, only while something is under way, what that needs: the invite you arrived with, the email address or phone number a sign-in code was sent to (for App Review's own account, which is sent no code, the address typed, until its sign-in page opens), the checks and the page to go back to for a sign-in with Google{% if apple_native %} or Apple{% endif %}, or a note of an exit the journal just recorded, until the journal shows it. It is signed so that it cannot be changed, but it is not encrypted.{% if apple_ready %} While you sign in with Apple on the website, a second cookie of Orenth's lasts ten minutes and holds only that sign-in's checks, the page to go back to and any invite.{% endif %}
  • No copies of your pages on your device. So that Orenth can open and say so plainly when there is no connection, your browser keeps the site's style sheet, its icons and a "no connection" page, which are the same for everybody. Every page with anything of yours on it is sent marked not to be stored, so it comes from the server each time and is not kept.

Keeping and deleting

Your data stays until you remove it. Your journal and each of its exits, your saved theses and your analysis history can be downloaded as CSV or JSON at any time, from the Account and Settings pages{% if in_app %} of the Orenth website, in a browser{% endif %}, without asking anyone. Deleting your account removes your trades, watchlist, alerts, theses, analysis history, shared pages, sign-in records and the devices you turned notifications on for straight away; the shared strategy record stays, because it was never yours alone and contains nothing about you.{% if apple_native %} If you signed in with Apple, it also asks Apple to end Orenth's access to your Apple Account.{% endif %} {% if iap_on %}It deletes the token that tied any Pro bought through Apple to your account, and keeps the rest of that subscription's record only as said above, linked to no account. It cannot cancel that subscription: Apple keeps billing until you cancel it in your iPhone's Settings > Apple Account > Subscriptions, which the Account page says before you delete.{% endif %}

The server keeps backups so that a fault cannot lose everyone's journal. A backup made before you deleted your account still holds a copy until it is replaced by newer ones, which takes about a week. The server also copies the database each time a new version of Orenth starts, and keeps the last {{ release.keep }} of those copies{% if release.days %}, deleting each after {{ release.days }} days{% else %}; one made before you deleted your account can stay until newer versions replace it, however long that takes{% endif %}. {% if offsite.provider %}Copies of the backups are also kept off this server: the newest from each of the last {{ offsite.days }} days and the oldest from each of the last {{ offsite.months }} months. So a copy made before you deleted your account can last up to {{ offsite.months }} months there, and if copies stop being sent there, those already there stay until they are deleted by hand.{% endif %}

Sessions expire after {{ session_days }} days. Signing out ends the session on the server rather than only in the browser.

Security

Google sign-in uses the authorization code flow with PKCE, exchanged between servers.{% if apple_native %} Apple sign-in checks Apple's signature on every token against Apple's published keys, and ties each token to the sign-in that asked for it.{% endif %} Code sign-in limits how many codes one address and one network can ask for, locks a code after five wrong tries, stops taking codes for an address after twenty wrong tries in a day, and caps how many texts can be sent in an hour in total. Session tokens are random, opaque and stored, so they can be revoked. Credentials are read from the environment and are never written to a configuration file.

What this is

Orenth is analysis software. It reads public market data, filings, news and social posts, applies a set of strategies to them, back-tests those strategies against the instrument's own history, and reports what it found.

What it is not

It is not financial advice, and nothing it produces is a recommendation to buy or sell anything. It does not know your circumstances, your tax position, your other holdings or your obligations, and it is not regulated to advise you about any of them. It has no connection to any broker and never places an order.

What the numbers mean

Every probability, expectancy and confidence interval is measured from historical price data. Past behaviour is not a promise about the future, and a measured edge can stop existing without warning. The software says so in its own output: when a setup has no demonstrated edge it reports that rather than inventing confidence, and by its own measurements most short-horizon trading loses money once costs are charged.

Day trading loses money for the large majority of retail participants. This tool's own back-tests agree with that finding. Never risk money you cannot afford to lose.

Data accuracy

Market data comes from third parties and may be delayed, incomplete or wrong. Figures taken from filings are shown with a link to the filing so you can check them. No warranty is given that any figure is correct, and decisions made on them are yours.

Availability

The service may be unavailable, and scheduled checks do not run while it is. Alerts are a convenience, not a guarantee: never rely on one to exit a position.
